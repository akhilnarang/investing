Reading this honestly

  • A falling rupee is not a return. If the rupee halves against the dollar over twenty years while US prices double, a dollar-holder's purchasing power in America is flat. The nominal number flatters; the inflation-adjusted one is the answer.
  • Cash is the floor, not the benchmark. Holding any currency and doing nothing with it is the worst version of every option here. The overnight rate line shows what the same rupees would have done sitting in the safest Indian instrument that pays anything at all.
  • The dirham and the riyal are pegged to the dollar, at 3.6725 and 3.75, so their rupee rates are the dollar's divided by a constant. They move because the dollar does, not because of anything happening in the Gulf.
  • Inflation here is annual and coarse. The World Bank publishes one figure a year, spread evenly across its months, and the current year carries the last published rate. That is the right grain for a twenty-year question and the wrong grain for anything shorter.

Where the numbers come from

  • Rates are the ECB's daily reference fixes, published at 14:15 CET, converted through the euro. They are not the rate your bank gives you: a retail conversion loses 1 to 3% to the spread and the fee, each way.
  • Month-end points only. The question is about years.
  • Inflation is the World Bank's CPI series for each country. The Indian one is MOSPI's, the American one is the BLS's.
  • Exchange rates and past inflation only. Not investment advice, and not a view on any currency.
